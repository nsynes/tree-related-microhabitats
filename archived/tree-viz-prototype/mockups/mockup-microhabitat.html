📖 Description

Rot holes are cavities in the trunk or branches caused by internal decay of heartwood. They typically form where branches have broken off or where wounds have allowed fungi to enter the tree. These features are extremely valuable for wildlife, providing secure nesting and roosting sites that are increasingly rare in managed woodlands. Rot holes can take decades or centuries to develop, making trees with these features particularly important for conservation.

Formation

Forms when decay fungi break down the heartwood through branch stubs, wounds, or natural aging processes. Development can take 20-100+ years depending on tree species and conditions.

Typical Size

Entrance diameter: 5-40 cm
Internal cavity: Variable, can extend deep into trunk
Often larger internally than entrance suggests

Location on Tree

Most common on trunk and major branches, often at old branch junctions. Particularly frequent on veteran trees and ancient specimens.

Associated Species

Oak is especially prone to forming rot holes due to heartwood susceptibility to decay fungi. Beech, ash, and willow also commonly develop these features.

🔍 Identification Tips

  • Look for dark openings in trunk or major branches
  • May have irregular edges with exposed decayed wood
  • Often shows evidence of use (droppings, nesting material, wear marks)
  • Interior usually dark brown to black from decay
  • Surrounding bark may be loose or showing fungal fruiting bodies
  • Distinguished from woodpecker holes by irregular shape and decay evidence

🌳 Conservation Importance

Rot holes are among the most valuable microhabitat features for biodiversity. They provide:

  • Critical nesting sites for cavity-nesting birds that cannot excavate their own holes
  • Bat roosts with stable temperature and humidity
  • Hibernation sites for invertebrates and small mammals
  • Specialized habitats for saproxylic (wood-decay) insects that are often rare
